Epiplexity

This is a note summarizing the manuscript “From Entropy to Epiplexity: Rethinking Information for Computationally Bounded Intelligence” by Finzi et al (2026) from arXiv.

Author

Hyejung Lee

Published

August 22, 2026

Studying the paper “From Entropy to Perplexity: Rethinking Information for Computationally Bounded Intelligence” by Finzi et al (2026) from arXiv.

2 Summary

Information theory approach to modeling.

3 Foundational Concepts

  • Shannon entropy: A basic way to quantify information is by how unpredictable a random outcome is. Shannon showed that if an event has probability \(p\), the “surprise” or information from seeing that event is \(-\log_2 p\) (in bits), and the entropy of a random source is the average surprise. For example, a fair coin flip has entropy 1 bit (maximally unpredictable), while a biased coin (say 70% heads) has less entropy. Intuitively, Shannon entropy measures how many bits on average you’d need to encode or describe samples from a distribution. Uniform, random data have high entropy (hard to compress), while very predictable or redundant data have low entropy.
  • Kolmogorov complexity: Another view of “information” is the length of the shortest computer program (in some fixed language) that produces a given data string as output. For instance, the string ababababab... can be described by a short loop (“print ‘ab’ 16 times”), so it has low Kolmogorov complexity. But a long string of random letters has no shorter description than just writing it out, so it has high complexity. Kolmogorov complexity thus captures how compressible or “structured” data are, in an absolute sense, if you allow unlimited computation.
  • Pseudorandomness: In practice, many “random-looking” sequences are actually produced by deterministic algorithms (pseudorandom generators). A pseudorandom sequence is one that appears statistically random, even though it is generated by a fixed procedure. For example, a good random number generator in software takes a short seed (say 128 bits) and deterministically produces millions of “random” bits. To any limited observer (say a program that checks basic statistical patterns), these bits look just like truly random bits. In cryptography, we formalize this by requiring that no efficient (polynomial-time) algorithm can tell apart the generator’s output from true randomness. In effect, to a bounded observer without the seed, a cryptographic PRNG output is random – all simple patterns are hidden.
  • Turing machines and computation: A Turing machine is a theoretical model of a computer: it has an infinite tape of memory and a simple rule table, but it can simulate any algorithm given enough time. We often use Turing machines (or equivalent formal models) to reason about what can be computed and how much time (or space) it takes. Importantly, a Turing machine with infinite time can simulate anything we can program, but with limited time it might not be able to find hidden patterns. The idea of a Turing machine sets the stage for distinguishing between what exists in principle (unbounded computation) versus what a real learner can feasibly do in practice.
  • Cryptography basics: Cryptography often relies on one-way functions: easy to compute in one direction, hard to invert without a key. A classic example is multiplying two large primes: easy to do, but factoring the product back into primes is believed to be hard for any efficient algorithm. Likewise, modern block ciphers and PRNGs use secret keys to produce outputs that look random unless you have the key. This illustrates how limited computation (no key, short time) makes something appear random that is actually structured. In effect, cryptographic ideas show that what counts as unpredictable or “random” depends on the observer’s power.

In summary, Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity are two classic “information” measures: entropy cares about unpredictability in a known distribution, and Kolmogorov complexity cares about shortest descriptions under unlimited computation. Pseudorandomness and cryptography remind us that if an observer can’t run a long algorithm (like factoring or breaking a cipher), a deterministic output can be effectively random. These ideas set the stage for asking: What is truly informative to a learner with limited compute?

4 Bounded Computation and Information

Traditional information measures assume an all-powerful observer. Shannon’s theory assumes you know the data distribution exactly, and Kolmogorov complexity assumes you have unlimited time to find the shortest program. But real learners (humans or neural nets) are computationally bounded – they only have so much time and memory. Once we account for that, what counts as information changes.

  • New “randomness” emerges: A classic example is pseudorandom numbers. The string from a good PRNG has high Shannon entropy and high Kolmogorov complexity (a short program generates it). Yet a limited agent sees no patterns: it looks like noise. In information-theoretic terms, its time-bounded entropy is almost maximal, but its structured content is nearly zero. That is, despite coming from a simple generator, any model that runs in realistic time cannot exploit its hidden simplicity. The paper shows that for a cryptographically secure generator, the epiplexity (new measure of structure) is basically constant, even though traditional entropy is huge.
  • One-way functions: Similarly, consider two data orderings like “(primes, product)” vs “(product, primes)”. Shannon information tells us the total info is the same. But an efficient learner given the product first must solve factoring (hard) before seeing the primes, so it gains nothing, whereas seeing primes first makes the product predictable. In practice, models learn much better in one ordering. Cryptography formalizes this: knowing “\(x, y\) → \(xy\)” vs “\(xy\) → \(x,y\)” are very different for bounded agents. In fact, the paper proves that if a one-way permutation exists, time-bounded entropy can differ significantly between the two orders.
  • Chaotic systems: Even simple deterministic systems (like the Lorenz equations or cellular automata) can produce behavior that is unpredictable without simulation. To a bounded observer, such a system generates a mix of randomness and structure: the state might look random moment-to-moment, but over time stable patterns emerge (like the Lorenz attractor). This means limited-time learners see partial predictability embedded in apparent chaos.

In short, bounded computation means some bits of data are effectively random to the learner, even if in principle they come from a short program. We must redefine information from the perspective of what a real algorithm can compute. This sets up the notion of epiplexity: measuring the learnable structure in data under a time limit, as opposed to total Kolmogorov complexity or Shannon entropy.

5 Epiplexity: The New Measure of Learnable Structure

Epiplexity (short for epistemic complexity) is Finzi et al.’s formalization of “how much structure a bounded learner can extract.” Roughly, it asks: If a learner with limited compute tries to compress or model the data, how much program bits does it need? Equivalently, it’s the size of the smallest model (in bits) that fits the data within the allowed computation time.

Concretely, they define epiplexity \(S_T(X)\) (for time bound \(T\)) via a two-part Minimum Description Length (MDL) approach:

The learner picks a fast-running program \(P\) (the model) that best predicts the data \(X\).

The model code length is \(|P|\) (bits to describe the program).

The data code length is how many bits to encode the remaining unpredictability (essentially \(-\log P(X)\)).

The epiplexity \(S_T(X)\) is the length \(|P^|\) of the shortest program that achieves optimal compression within time \(T\). The leftover bits (average \(-\log P^(X)\)) is called the time-bounded entropy \(H_T(X)\). Intuitively:

\(S_T(X)\) (epiplexity) measures the structured, predictable content: how large a model the learner must build to capture patterns in \(X\).

\(H_T(X)\) (time-bounded entropy) measures the inherently random part: the bits the model can’t predict and must encode as pure surprise.

For a truly random string (like fair coin flips), a trivial model (the “always uniform” model) explains it, so \(S_T\) is small (constant) even though \(H_T\) is large. Conversely, for very structured data (e.g. repeated patterns or images of objects), \(S_T\) can be large because the model must capture all that regularity, even if the data have lower Shannon entropy.

This splits classical information into “structure you can learn” versus “noise you can’t”, given the learner’s limits. For example, the output of a secure PRNG has tiny epiplexity (no learnable structure) but huge unpredictability. On the other hand, two successive snapshots of a chaotic system might allow learning some underlying rule (increasing epiplexity) even though each step is unpredictable. Essentially, epiplexity captures the structural information available to a time-bounded observer, whereas Shannon entropy captures the remaining randomness.

6 The Three Paradoxes of Classical Information

The paper identifies three “paradoxes” where classical theory seems to say one thing, yet practice shows another:

  1. Deterministic Transformations can’t create new information: Shannon’s data processing inequality and algorithmic information theory both say: if you take data \(X\) and compute \(Y=f(X)\) by any deterministic algorithm, \(Y\) cannot contain more information about the world than \(X\) did (formally \(H(Y)\le H(X)\), or \(K(f(x))\le K(x)+K(f)\)). In other words, you can’t “manufacture” new information out of thin air by just processing old data. Yet in practice, we see the opposite: running a chess engine (AlphaZero) or a simulation on simple game rules yields a model that seems to have learned far more – sophisticated strategies, evaluations, etc. Synthetic data pipelines (deterministic transforms of random noise) can improve a learner. Mathematicians derive novel theorems from simple axioms. These experiences suggest new “useful” information is created, contradicting classical theory.
  2. Information is independent of data order (factorization): Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity are symmetric in the sense that the total information in \((X,Y)\) is the same regardless of whether you view it as “\(X\) then \(Y\)” or “\(Y\) then \(X\)” (formally \(H(X,Y)=H(X|Y)+H(Y)=H(Y|X)+H(X)\)). That is, swapping order shouldn’t change total content. However, modern models pick up on an “arrow of time”: for example, English text is much easier to model left-to-right than right-to-left, and self-supervised models often learn better in the original temporal order than a shuffled one. Cryptography even exploits this: concatenating \((x,y)\) vs \((y,x)\) can dramatically change learnability if \(y=f(x)\) with a one-way function. So the order in which information is presented can affect what a bounded learner actually picks up, which seems at odds with classical invariance.
  3. Likelihood modeling is just distribution matching: A common belief is that maximum likelihood training can at best “recover” the true data distribution. If the real data come from a simple process \(Q\), then the best model is just \(Q\) itself; you can’t learn any further structure that wasn’t in \(Q\). In other words, training a model should be equivalent to identifying (or matching) the data-generating distribution, with no extra emergent insights. But in practice we see phenomena like induction and emergence: models often learn rules or circuits that the data generator didn’t explicitly provide. For example, Conway’s Game of Life has a tiny rule-table (the “genesis” of data), but observers spot gliders, spaceships, and other emergent objects that were not coded in the rules. Language models trained on text learn grammar and semantics beyond the raw text. This suggests that the act of learning can produce models that are more complex (in structure) than the simple simulator that generated the data.

These three points create a tension: classical information theory suggests you can’t invent new information by processing data, that order doesn’t matter, and that model learning is just distribution fitting. Yet empirical practice shows we often do “get more” out of data (useful structure, order effects, emergent abilities) than the classical measures predict.

7 How Epiplexity Resolves the Paradoxes

Epiplexity and time-bounded entropy reconcile these puzzles by explicitly taking computation into account:

  1. Paradox 1 (Deterministic “creation” of info): In epiplexity terms, a deterministic process can generate extra time-bounded entropy or structure that a learner can extract. For instance, a PRNG clearly has a deterministic seed, but from a bounded observer’s view it creates randomness (high \(H_T\)) without increasing \(S_T\). Conversely, running a complex simulation (like self-play in AlphaZero or unfolding a cellular automaton) can produce new learnable structure (increasing \(S_T\)) even though no Shannon information was added. The paper demonstrates that simulated dynamical systems (e.g. cellular automata or Lorenz equations) can generate patterns (“gliders,” attractors) that make \(S_T\) go up with compute. In other words, epiplexity does not obey the classical data-processing inequality for a bounded observer: extra computation (even deterministic) can effectively increase the information seen by the model. This matches intuition: given more processing, a learner can extract new regularities that were not apparent in the raw data.
  2. Paradox 2 (Order matters): Epiplexity acknowledges that how we factor or order data affects what patterns are detectable within time. As the paper explains, in a time-bounded setting a short description of \(f(x)\) does not imply a short description of \(f^{-1}(x)\). Concretely, if \(Y=f(X)\) with a one-way \(f\), then \(S_T(Y)+H_T(Y|X)\) and \(S_T(X)+H_T(X|Y)\) can differ. The authors prove that for a true one-way permutation, the time-bounded entropies are uneven: one ordering yields significantly higher entropy (harder to predict) than the reverse. Empirically, they show that swapping the order of chess game data (moves then final board vs final board then moves) changes the model’s ability and the epiplexity. In short, epiplexity depends on factorization, so different orders can indeed yield different “amounts of learnable information,” resolving the symmetry paradox.
  3. Paradox 3 (Likelihood vs learning): Under epiplexity, learning by likelihood can produce models whose structure is far richer than the original generator. Because a bounded learner may have to do extra inference (e.g. fill in missing bits) to predict the data, the optimal model can be much larger. The paper shows that there is no fixed constant relating the epiplexity of a generated output to the size of the generating program. In other words, even if the data come from a simple program \(G\), the best model \(P^*\) might have much higher complexity if the learner must “work harder.” They formalize this as a Limited Epiplexity Increase property failing – there is no universal bound \(c\) such that \(S_T(G(Z))\le |G|+c\) for all programs \(G\). Practically, training a model can force it to invent new internal “circuits” (like solving a hidden puzzle) that were not in the data generator. For example, to maximize likelihood in certain “induction” tasks, the model must effectively run an exhaustive search (a bigger program) to infer missing information, boosting epiplexity. Thus, epiplexity allows a learned model to be more complex than the data source, explaining how models can “discover” structure that Shannon-theory would say isn’t there.

n summary, by distinguishing learnable structure from inherent randomness, epiplexity shows that computation itself can transform information. Deterministic algorithms can produce additional patterns (raising \(S_T\)), ordering can change predictability (changing \(S_T\) and \(H_T\)), and learning procedures can build extra structure beyond the raw distribution. These effects all become natural once we drop the assumption of infinite computing power.

8 Estimating Epiplexity in Practice

To make epiplexity practical, Finzi et al. propose proxy measurements based on training real models. Two main approaches are given:

  • Training loss curves (area above final loss): When a model (e.g. a neural net) trains on data, its training loss typically decreases over time. The area under the loss curve above its final value quantifies how much “bits” of information the model gradually learned from the data. Intuitively, if training sharply decreases loss and flattens quickly, the area (hence epiplexity) is small; but if loss keeps dropping steadily for a long time, the model is absorbing a lot of structure. In experiments, the authors actually compute this area (using cross-entropy loss) as a simple heuristic for epiplexity.
  • Teacher–student coding (cumulative KL divergence): A more formal method uses a “prequential” or sequential coding view. Imagine a teacher model (the data distribution) generating examples, and a student model being updated after each example. The information gained at each step is the KL-divergence (or change in log-loss) between the student’s prediction and the true next token. Summing these gains over training equals how many bits the student learned overall. This cumulative KL can be interpreted as a two-part code length (one part for the learned model, one for the data), closely approximating epiplexity. In practice, computing the exact KL to the true distribution is hard, but one can approximate by comparing successive checkpoints (“teacher” vs “student” checkpoints) or by measuring regret under a sequential code.

Both methods require only monitoring standard training outputs (loss vs steps). For example, the authors train language models on different synthetic datasets (cellular automata, Markov chains, hidden induction tasks) and measure the epiplexity via these techniques. They find that datasets with more hidden structure cause larger losses early on and more gradual learning, leading to a larger area under the curve. Thus these procedures give concrete estimates of the structural information in the data.

9 Implications for Machine Learning

The concept of epiplexity has several practical insights for ML:

  • Synthetic data: Synthetic or simulated data is often used to augment training. Classical theory (data-processing) suggests a deterministic synthetic pipeline shouldn’t add any new information beyond the original random seed. In contrast, epiplexity predicts that well-designed synthetic data can raise structural information for the model. For instance, creating data that draws out certain patterns (changing order, interpolating between examples, or simulating a process) can increase epiplexity. The authors argue that this explains why synthetic data helps model capabilities. Epiplexity provides a principled guide: synthetic data should be chosen to maximize learnable structure, not just match distributions.
  • Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization: Models often succeed on new tasks only if their training data had reusable structure. High-epiplexity data tend to encode rich features that transfer to other domains. For example, text corpora yield higher epiplexity than raw pixel images, which may explain why language-pretrained models generalize broadly. The authors show empirically that datasets with higher measured epiplexity lead to better OOD performance. In other words, epiplexity quantifies the “value” of data for future tasks: data that induces more structural circuits in the model (higher \(S_T\)) are more useful beyond their original domain.
  • Data selection and curriculum: Since epiplexity can be estimated from loss curves, it offers a new criterion for selecting or generating data. Instead of just balancing distributions or picking “hard” examples by error, one could choose data that maximizes epiplexity (drives large sustained learning). For instance, the paper discusses how certain adaptive data algorithms inadvertently favor high-epiplexity examples, leading to better generalization. In the long run, epiplexity could guide curriculum design: sequence or filter data to increase the model’s structural knowledge efficiently.
  • Understanding models: Measuring epiplexity helps explain model behaviors like induction or emergent abilities. If a task has hidden bits the model must infer, training loss curves will reflect high epiplexity (as in the rule-30 and Markov chain examples in Figure 5). Conversely, tasks where the information is already “open-book” lead to low epiplexity. This perspective helps practitioners ask: How much true structure must the model learn? and choose data accordingly.

In summary, epiplexity shifts focus from model complexity to data complexity in a compute-sensitive way. It provides a theoretical basis for “value of data” questions: how to construct or pick data that injects new learnable information into a system. Unlike traditional model-selection criteria, it suggests data-selection principles: pick or generate datasets that maximize epiplexity under your compute budget. This could improve synthetic data pipelines, domain adaptation, and generalization in future ML systems.

Mathematicians derive novel theorems from simple axioms. These experiences suggest new “useful” information is created, contradicting classical theory.
2. **Information is independent of data order (factorization)**: Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity are symmetric in the sense that the total information in $(X,Y)$ is the same regardless of whether you view it as “$X$ then $Y$” or “$Y$ then $X$” (formally $H(X,Y)=H(X|Y)+H(Y)=H(Y|X)+H(X)$). That is, swapping order shouldn’t change total content. However, modern models pick up on an “arrow of time”: for example, English text is much easier to model left-to-right than right-to-left, and self-supervised models often learn better in the original temporal order than a shuffled one. Cryptography even exploits this: concatenating $(x,y)$ vs $(y,x)$ can dramatically change learnability if $y=f(x)$ with a one-way function. So the order in which information is presented can affect what a bounded learner actually picks up, which seems at odds with classical invariance.
3. **Likelihood modeling is just distribution matching**: A common belief is that maximum likelihood training can at best “recover” the true data distribution. If the real data come from a simple process $Q$, then the best model is just $Q$ itself; you can’t learn any further structure that wasn’t in $Q$. In other words, training a model should be equivalent to identifying (or matching) the data-generating distribution, with no extra emergent insights. But in practice we see phenomena like induction and emergence: models often learn rules or circuits that the data generator didn’t explicitly provide. For example, Conway’s Game of Life has a tiny rule-table (the “genesis” of data), but observers spot gliders, spaceships, and other emergent objects that were not coded in the rules. Language models trained on text learn grammar and semantics beyond the raw text. This suggests that the act of learning can produce models that are more complex (in structure) than the simple simulator that generated the data.


These three points create a tension: classical information theory suggests you can’t invent new information by processing data, that order doesn’t matter, and that model learning is just distribution fitting. Yet empirical practice shows we often do “get more” out of data (useful structure, order effects, emergent abilities) than the classical measures predict.


# How Epiplexity Resolves the Paradoxes

Epiplexity and time-bounded entropy reconcile these puzzles by explicitly taking computation into account:

1. **Paradox 1 (Deterministic “creation” of info)**: In epiplexity terms, a deterministic process can generate extra time-bounded entropy or structure that a learner can extract. For instance, a PRNG clearly has a deterministic seed, but from a bounded observer’s view it creates randomness (high $H_T$) without increasing $S_T$. Conversely, running a complex simulation (like self-play in AlphaZero or unfolding a cellular automaton) can produce new learnable structure (increasing $S_T$) even though no Shannon information was added. The paper demonstrates that simulated dynamical systems (e.g. cellular automata or Lorenz equations) can generate patterns (“gliders,” attractors) that make $S_T$ go up with compute. In other words, epiplexity does not obey the classical data-processing inequality for a bounded observer: extra computation (even deterministic) can effectively increase the information seen by the model. This matches intuition: given more processing, a learner can extract new regularities that were not apparent in the raw data.
2. **Paradox 2 (Order matters)**: Epiplexity acknowledges that how we factor or order data affects what patterns are detectable within time. As the paper explains, in a time-bounded setting a short description of $f(x)$ does not imply a short description of $f^{-1}(x)$. Concretely, if $Y=f(X)$ with a one-way $f$, then $S_T(Y)+H_T(Y|X)$ and $S_T(X)+H_T(X|Y)$ can differ. The authors prove that for a true one-way permutation, the time-bounded entropies are uneven: one ordering yields significantly higher entropy (harder to predict) than the reverse. Empirically, they show that swapping the order of chess game data (moves then final board vs final board then moves) changes the model’s ability and the epiplexity. In short, epiplexity depends on factorization, so different orders can indeed yield different “amounts of learnable information,” resolving the symmetry paradox.
3. **Paradox 3 (Likelihood vs learning)**: Under epiplexity, learning by likelihood can produce models whose structure is far richer than the original generator. Because a bounded learner may have to do extra inference (e.g. fill in missing bits) to predict the data, the optimal model can be much larger. The paper shows that there is no fixed constant relating the epiplexity of a generated output to the size of the generating program. In other words, even if the data come from a simple program $G$, the best model $P^*$ might have much higher complexity if the learner must “work harder.” They formalize this as a Limited Epiplexity Increase property failing – there is no universal bound $c$ such that $S_T(G(Z))\le |G|+c$ for all programs $G$. Practically, training a model can force it to invent new internal “circuits” (like solving a hidden puzzle) that were not in the data generator. For example, to maximize likelihood in certain “induction” tasks, the model must effectively run an exhaustive search (a bigger program) to infer missing information, boosting epiplexity. Thus, epiplexity allows a learned model to be more complex than the data source, explaining how models can “discover” structure that Shannon-theory would say isn’t there.

n summary, by distinguishing learnable structure from inherent randomness, epiplexity shows that computation itself can transform information. Deterministic algorithms can produce additional patterns (raising $S_T$), ordering can change predictability (changing $S_T$ and $H_T$), and learning procedures can build extra structure beyond the raw distribution. These effects all become natural once we drop the assumption of infinite computing power.

# Estimating Epiplexity in Practice

To make epiplexity practical, Finzi et al. propose proxy measurements based on training real models. Two main approaches are given:

- Training loss curves (area above final loss): When a model (e.g. a neural net) trains on data, its training loss typically decreases over time. The area under the loss curve above its final value quantifies how much “bits” of information the model gradually learned from the data. Intuitively, if training sharply decreases loss and flattens quickly, the area (hence epiplexity) is small; but if loss keeps dropping steadily for a long time, the model is absorbing a lot of structure. In experiments, the authors actually compute this area (using cross-entropy loss) as a simple heuristic for epiplexity.
- Teacher–student coding (cumulative KL divergence): A more formal method uses a “prequential” or sequential coding view. Imagine a teacher model (the data distribution) generating examples, and a student model being updated after each example. The information gained at each step is the KL-divergence (or change in log-loss) between the student’s prediction and the true next token. Summing these gains over training equals how many bits the student learned overall. This cumulative KL can be interpreted as a two-part code length (one part for the learned model, one for the data), closely approximating epiplexity. In practice, computing the exact KL to the true distribution is hard, but one can approximate by comparing successive checkpoints (“teacher” vs “student” checkpoints) or by measuring regret under a sequential code.

Both methods require only monitoring standard training outputs (loss vs steps). For example, the authors train language models on different synthetic datasets (cellular automata, Markov chains, hidden induction tasks) and measure the epiplexity via these techniques. They find that datasets with more hidden structure cause larger losses early on and more gradual learning, leading to a larger area under the curve. Thus these procedures give concrete estimates of the structural information in the data.


# Implications for Machine Learning

The concept of epiplexity has several practical insights for ML:

- **Synthetic data**: Synthetic or simulated data is often used to augment training. Classical theory (data-processing) suggests a deterministic synthetic pipeline shouldn’t add any new information beyond the original random seed. In contrast, epiplexity predicts that well-designed synthetic data can raise structural information for the model. For instance, creating data that draws out certain patterns (changing order, interpolating between examples, or simulating a process) can increase epiplexity. The authors argue that this explains why synthetic data helps model capabilities. Epiplexity provides a principled guide: synthetic data should be chosen to maximize learnable structure, not just match distributions.
- **Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization**: Models often succeed on new tasks only if their training data had reusable structure. High-epiplexity data tend to encode rich features that transfer to other domains. For example, text corpora yield higher epiplexity than raw pixel images, which may explain why language-pretrained models generalize broadly. The authors show empirically that datasets with higher measured epiplexity lead to better OOD performance. In other words, epiplexity quantifies the “value” of data for future tasks: data that induces more structural circuits in the model (higher $S_T$) are more useful beyond their original domain.
- **Data selection and curriculum**: Since epiplexity can be estimated from loss curves, it offers a new criterion for selecting or generating data. Instead of just balancing distributions or picking “hard” examples by error, one could choose data that maximizes epiplexity (drives large sustained learning). For instance, the paper discusses how certain adaptive data algorithms inadvertently favor high-epiplexity examples, leading to better generalization. In the long run, epiplexity could guide curriculum design: sequence or filter data to increase the model’s structural knowledge efficiently.
- **Understanding model**s: Measuring epiplexity helps explain model behaviors like induction or emergent abilities. If a task has hidden bits the model must infer, training loss curves will reflect high epiplexity (as in the rule-30 and Markov chain examples in Figure 5). Conversely, tasks where the information is already “open-book” lead to low epiplexity. This perspective helps practitioners ask: How much true structure must the model learn? and choose data accordingly.


In summary, epiplexity shifts focus from model complexity to data complexity in a compute-sensitive way. It provides a theoretical basis for “value of data” questions: how to construct or pick data that injects new learnable information into a system. Unlike traditional model-selection criteria, it suggests data-selection principles: pick or generate datasets that maximize epiplexity under your compute budget. This could improve synthetic data pipelines, domain adaptation, and generalization in future ML systems.
