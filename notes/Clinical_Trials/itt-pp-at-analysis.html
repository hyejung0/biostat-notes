Intention-to-treat vs. per-protocol vs. as-treated analyses in clinical trials

clinical-trial
causal-inference

Clinical trials often use different analytical approaches to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions. This article explores the differences among intention-to-treat (ITT), per-protocol (PP), and as-treated (AT) analyses, highlighting their implications for interpreting trial results and making informed decisions in clinical practice.

Author

Hyejung Lee

Published

August 22, 2026

1 Background

Nonadherence is a common challenge in randomized clinical trials (RCTs). It occurs when participants do not follow the randomly assigned treatment protocol — for example, by not taking trial medications, crossing over to the other intervention, or accessing treatment outside the trial1. When nonadherence is substantial, different analytic strategies can yield materially different estimates of treatment effect, each answering a subtly different causal question.

The three most commonly used approaches are:

  • Intention-to-treat (ITT): analyze all participants as originally randomized
  • Per-protocol (PP): analyze only participants who adhered to the protocol
  • As-treated (AT): analyze participants according to the treatment they actually received

Understanding what each approach estimates — and where each can go wrong — is essential for interpreting trial results and translating them into clinical practice2,3.

2 Causal Estimands: What Are We Trying to Estimate?

Before comparing the three approaches, it helps to distinguish the two causal effects we might care about2:

Effect of assigned treatment (Z → Y)
The average causal effect of the treatment assignment on the outcome. This is what an ITT analysis estimates. It captures real-world effectiveness including any dilution due to nonadherence.
Effect of received treatment (A → Y)
The average causal effect of the treatment actually taken on the outcome. This is what PP and AT analyses try to estimate. It reflects what would happen if all participants fully adhered.

The two effects coincide only when adherence is perfect (i.e., Z = A for every participant).

3 The Three Approaches: Definitions

3.1 Intention-to-Treat (ITT)

In an ITT analysis, all randomized participants are included in the analysis in the groups to which they were originally assigned, regardless of what treatment they actually received1,3.

  • Population included: All randomized participants
  • Treatment variable: Assigned treatment (Z)
  • Handles nonadherence by: Keeping participants in their assigned group regardless of crossover

3.2 Per-Protocol (PP)

A PP analysis restricts the analytic sample to participants who adhered to the trial protocol as specified. In the time-varying setting, this typically means censoring participants at the time they deviate from their assigned treatment (e.g., 6 months after nonadherence begins)2,4.

  • Population included: Adherent participants (or adherent person-time)
  • Treatment variable: Assigned treatment (Z) in the adherent subgroup
  • Handles nonadherence by: Exclusion (or censoring at deviation)

3.3 As-Treated (AT)

An AT analysis classifies participants according to the treatment they actually received, ignoring their randomization assignment. It treats RCT data as if they were observational1,2.

  • Population included: All participants
  • Treatment variable: Received treatment (A), possibly time-varying
  • Handles nonadherence by: Reclassifying participants by actual exposure

5 Strengths and Limitations

5.1 ITT

Strengths

  • Preserves the benefits of randomization, eliminating confounding of the treatment–outcome association
  • Provides a valid test of the null hypothesis of no treatment effect in double-blind trials2
  • Simple to implement and interpret; no modeling assumptions required for confounding

Limitations

  • Estimates the effect of assignment, not of treatment. The estimate is diluted toward null when nonadherence is substantial1,2
  • Anti-conservative for safety trials and non-inferiority trials: a null or small ITT effect does not rule out a true harmful or inferior treatment2
  • In active-comparator trials, ITT can overestimate a treatment’s benefit if adherence is differential across arms (see Scenario 2 in2)
  • The estimate depends on the trial-specific adherence pattern and may not generalize to settings where adherence differs4
  • Many “ITT” analyses in practice are modified (e.g., restricted to patients who received at least one dose), which can introduce selection bias1

5.2 Per-Protocol (PP)

Strengths

  • Targets the effect of treatment under full adherence, which is often the clinically relevant question for a patient who intends to adhere
  • Provides an upper bound on treatment effect in many placebo-controlled settings
  • Particularly useful when the trial-specific adherence pattern is not representative of clinical practice4

Limitations

  • Naive PP (simple restriction to adherent participants) is subject to selection bias: adherence is not random, and adherers may differ systematically from nonadherers in ways that also affect the outcome2
  • This selection bias can go in either direction and is generally hard to predict without formal adjustment
  • Requires measurement of time-varying confounders of adherence (and of the censoring process), making data collection more demanding
  • Validity rests on untestable assumptions about unmeasured confounders1

5.3 As-Treated (AT)

Strengths

  • Uses all available data and accounts for the actual treatment received
  • Can detect a larger treatment effect than ITT when adherence is imperfect, providing information about the “undiluted” effect1
  • May be informative when combined with ITT results to triangulate the range of possible effects

Limitations

  • Ignores randomization entirely; effectively converts the RCT into an observational study2
  • Subject to confounding: participants who choose to take (or not take) treatment may differ prognostically from those who do not
  • Time-varying confounders that are themselves affected by prior treatment require g-methods (IP weighting or g-estimation) for valid adjustment; conventional regression is insufficient2
  • Assumes that all confounders have been measured and correctly modeled — an untestable assumption

6 Adjustment Methods for PP and AT Analyses

When using PP or AT analyses, naive restriction or reclassification is rarely valid. The following methods have been developed to address confounding and selection bias2,4:

Inverse Probability (IP) Weighting
Creates a pseudo-population in which adherence is independent of confounders by weighting each participant’s contribution by the inverse of the probability of their observed adherence pattern. Can handle time-varying confounders affected by prior treatment.
G-Estimation (Structural Nested Models)
Estimates structural parameters that relate dose-response to the counterfactual outcome. Like IP weighting, it handles time-varying confounders appropriately.
Instrumental Variable (IV) Estimation
A form of g-estimation that uses the random treatment assignment (Z) as an instrument. It does not require measuring confounders, but requires a correctly specified structural model and a valid instrument. Most credible in double-blind trials.

All three approaches require untestable assumptions and careful data collection on time-varying confounders and adherence throughout follow-up. The choice of method should be prespecified in the trial protocol.

7 Worked Example: The CABANA Trial

The CABANA trial randomized patients with atrial fibrillation to either catheter ablation or conventional drug therapy1,5. Substantial crossover occurred: 9% of those assigned to ablation did not receive the procedure, and 27% of those assigned to drug therapy ultimately underwent ablation. This created a challenging analytic situation with nonadherence in both directions.

Results under each approach1:

Analysis HR (95% CI) Interpretation
ITT 0.86 (0.65–1.15) No significant reduction in primary composite outcome
Per-Protocol 0.74 (0.54–1.01) Larger effect estimate; borderline non-significant
As-Treated 0.67 (0.50–0.89) Statistically significant benefit of ablation

Taken together, these results suggest a possible beneficial effect of ablation when patients are able to undergo the procedure. However, the PP and AT estimates must be interpreted cautiously: patients unable to undergo ablation may have had worse post-randomization health status, which could bias results in favor of ablation1.

8 Practical Guidance for Analysts

The following workflow is recommended for trials with substantial nonadherence2,4:

  1. Always report an ITT analysis as the primary analysis. It provides an unbiased estimate of the effect of treatment assignment and preserves the benefits of randomization.

  2. Prespecify PP and/or AT analyses in the protocol, including:

    • The definition of adherence/nonadherence
    • The time-varying confounders that will be measured and how
    • The adjustment method (IP weighting, g-estimation, or IV estimation)

  3. Collect time-varying data on confounders and adherence throughout follow-up, not just at baseline. This is necessary for valid IP weighting and g-estimation.

  4. Be explicit about estimands: make clear whether you are estimating the effect of assigned treatment, the effect of continuous treatment, or the effect of some other clinically meaningful treatment strategy.

  5. Triangulate across analyses: discordance between ITT and PP/AT estimates is informative. Large differences suggest substantial nonadherence is biasing the ITT estimate; but large PP/AT effects may also reflect residual confounding.

  6. Avoid naive PP and AT analyses (simple restriction or reclassification without confounding adjustment). These should be treated with the same scrutiny as observational study estimates.

9 Summary

No single analysis is universally superior. The ITT analysis is not always conservative, and PP/AT analyses are not always biased. The appropriate approach depends on the causal question of interest, the pattern of nonadherence, and the quality of confounder measurement. Ideally, all three analyses should be reported and interpreted jointly1,2,4.
