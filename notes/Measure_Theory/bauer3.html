Measure Theory by Bauer Section 3: Contents, premeasure, measure

measure-theory

Section 3 of Heinz Bauer’s Measure and Integration Theory Translated from the German by Robert B. Burckel

Author

Hyejung Lee

Published

August 22, 2026

1 1. Why section 3 exists: “size” needs countable additivity

Elementary geometry suggests two core “calculation rules” for areas/volumes:

  • Finite additivity: if you split a shape into finitely many disjoint pieces, the total size is the sum of the piece sizes.
  • Countable additivity (\(\sigma\)-additivity): if you split into countably many disjoint pieces, the total size is the sum of the piece sizes.

Bauer motivates measure theory by emphasizing that the real power comes from the countable version—this is what makes limit procedures behave well (think of approximating a disk by polygons).

2 2. The set-systems in the background: rings vs \(\sigma\)-algebras

2.1 2.1 Ring (finite set-operations)

A ring of sets \(\mathcal{R}\subset\mathscr{P}(\Omega)\) is closed under:

  • \(A\setminus B\) (difference),
  • \(A\cup B\) (finite unions), and contains \(\emptyset\). (Bauer section 1, Def. 1.3).

Rings are where “finite decomposition” arguments live naturally.

2.2 2.2 \(\sigma\)-algebra (countable unions allowed)

A \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) is closed under complements and countable unions. (Bauer section 1, Def. 1.1).

\(\sigma\)-algebras are where “countable decomposition” is always meaningful, because \(\bigcup\limits_{n\ge 1} A_n\in\mathcal{F}\) whenever each \(A_n\in\mathcal{F}\).

3 3. Definition 3.1: premeasure vs content

Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be a ring in \(\Omega\). Let \(\mu:\mathcal{R}\to[0,+\infty]\).

3.1 3.1 Premeasure (\(\sigma\)-additivity on a ring, when the union stays in the ring)

\(\mu\) is a premeasure on \(\mathcal{R}\) if:

  1. \(\mu(\emptyset)=0\) \((3.1)\)

  2. For every pairwise disjoint sequence \((A_n)\subset\mathcal{R}\) whose union still lies in \(\mathcal{R}\),

    \[\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \tag{3.2}\]

This is Bauer’s Def. 3.1.

Why the extra clause “whose union lies in the ring”?
A ring is not closed under countable unions. So Bauer only requires (3.2) in the situations where both sides are defined inside the ring.

3.2 3.2 Content (finite additivity only)

\(\mu\) is a content if it satisfies (3.1) and instead of (3.2) only finite additivity:

\[\mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^n \mu(A_i) \quad\text{for disjoint }A_1,\dots,A_n\in\mathcal{R}. \tag{3.3}\]

This is Bauer’s Def. 3.1.

Every premeasure is a content, by taking \(A_{n+1}=A_{n+2}=\cdots=\emptyset\) in (3.2).

4 4. The key lesson: finite additivity is not enough

section 3 is designed to make you fluent in the hierarchy:

\[ \text{content} \quad \subsetneq \quad \text{premeasure} \quad \subsetneq \quad \text{measure}. \]

  • A content behaves well only under finite decompositions.
  • A premeasure behaves well under countable decompositions (where defined).
  • A measure is a premeasure whose domain is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, so countable additivity is always meaningful.

Bauer makes this precise in Definition 3.3 (see section 8 below).

5 5. Examples (especially 2 and 3)

5.1 Example 1 (unit mass / Dirac on a ring)

For \(\omega\in\Omega\), define on a ring \(\mathcal{R}\):

\[ \varepsilon_\omega(A)= \begin{cases} 1,& \omega\in A\\ 0,& \omega\notin A. \end{cases} \]

Bauer states \(\varepsilon_\omega\) is a premeasure (hence a content).

Intuition: “all mass concentrated at one point.”

5.2 Example 2 (0–1 measure on the countable/co-countable \(\sigma\)-algebra)

Setting. Let \(\Omega\) be uncountable (e.g. \(\Omega = \mathbb{R}\)) . Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the \(\sigma\)-algebra of all sets that are countable or co-countable (Example 2 in section 1).

Define

\[ \mu(A)= \begin{cases} 0,& A\text{ is countable}\\ 1,& A^c\text{ is countable (i.e. }A\text{ is co-countable)}. \end{cases} \]

Bauer: \(\mu\) is a premeasure on \(\mathcal{F}\).

5.2.1 Why \(\sigma\)-additivity holds (the essential mechanism)

Take disjoint \(A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{F}\).

  • At most one of them can be co-countable (have countable complement).
    Reason: if \(A_m\) is co-countable and \(A_n\cap A_m=\emptyset\), then \(A_n\subset A_m^c\), and \(A_m^c\) is countable, so \(A_n\) is countable.

So either:

  1. All \(A_n\) are countable ⇒ union is countable ⇒ \(\mu(\cup A_n)=0=\sum 0\); or
  2. Exactly one \(A_m\) is co-countable ⇒ \(\mu(A_m)=1\) and all others have \(\mu=0\); union is co-countable ⇒ \(\mu(\cup A_n)=1=\sum \mu(A_n)\).

Because \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, this \(\mu\) is not only a premeasure but (by Definition 3.3) a measure.

Interpretation. This measure declares:

  • “countable sets have mass 0,”
  • “everything else (co-countable sets) has mass 1.”

It is a legitimate measure on that \(\sigma\)-algebra, but not on the full power set \(\mathscr{P}(\Omega)\) in general.

5.3 Example 3 (0–1 content on the finite/co-finite algebra; not a premeasure)

Setting. Let \(\Omega\) be countably infinite (e.g. \(\Omega = \mathbb{N}\)). Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be the algebra of sets that are finite or co-finite (Example 9 in section 1).

Define

\[ \mu(A)= \begin{cases} 0,& A\text{ is finite}\\ 1,& A^c\text{ is finite (i.e. }A\text{ is co-finite)}. \end{cases} \]

Bauer: \(\mu\) is a content but not a premeasure.

5.3.1 (i) Why finite additivity holds

Let \(A_1,\dots,A_n\) be pairwise disjoint sets in \(\mathcal{A}\).

  • If one of them, say \(A_j\), is co-finite, then \(A_j^c\) is finite and for \(i\neq j\), \(A_i\subset A_j^c\), hence \(A_i\) is finite. So \(\sum \mu(A_i)=1\) and the union is co-finite ⇒ \(\mu(\cup A_i)=1\).
  • If none is co-finite, all are finite ⇒ union finite ⇒ \(\mu(\cup A_i)=0=\sum 0\).

So (3.3) holds: it is a content.

5.3.2 (ii) Why \(\sigma\)-additivity fails (and this is the crucial point)

Because \(\Omega\) is countably infinite, it can be written as a disjoint union of singletons:

\[ \Omega=\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{\omega_n\}. \]

Each singleton is finite, so \(\mu(\{\omega_n\})=0\) for all \(n\), hence

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(\{\omega_n\})=0. \]

But \(\bigcup_n \{\omega_n\}=\Omega\), and \(\Omega\) is co-finite in itself (complement \(\emptyset\) is finite), so \(\mu(\Omega)=1\). Therefore

\[ \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{\omega_n\}\right)=1\neq 0=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(\{\omega_n\}). \]

So (3.2) fails. Bauer explicitly points to exactly this decomposition as the reason.

Interpretation. Example 3 is the canonical warning:

A finitely additive “size” can look plausible on simple sets, but fail completely when you pass to countably many pieces.

5.4 Example 4 (linear combinations)

If \((\mu_n)\) are contents (or premeasures) on a ring \(\mathcal{R}\), and \((\alpha_n)\) are nonnegative real numbers, then

\[ \mu:=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \alpha_n\mu_n \]

is again a content (or premeasure) on \(\mathcal{R}\).

This “closure under nonnegative linear combination” is used frequently in probability (mixtures, superpositions).

6 6. Properties every content has (even before \(\sigma\)-additivity)

Bauer derives several identities/inequalities that any content \(\mu\) on a ring satisfies.

For \(A,B,A_1,\dots\in\mathcal{R}\):

  1. Inclusion–exclusion (two-set form)

\[ \mu(A\cup B)+\mu(A\cap B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B). \tag{3.5} \]

  1. Monotonicity (isotoneity)

\[ A\subset B \;\Rightarrow\; \mu(A)\le \mu(B). \tag{3.6} \]

  1. Subtractivity (when \(\mu(A)<\infty\))

\[ A\subset B,\ \mu(A)<\infty \Rightarrow \mu(B\setminus A)=\mu(B)-\mu(A). \tag{3.7} \]

  1. Finite subadditivity

\[ \mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right)\le \sum_{i=1}^n \mu(A_i). \tag{3.8} \]

  1. A one-sided inequality for disjoint sequences in a ring: for disjoint \((A_n)\subset\mathcal{R}\) with \(\bigcup_n A_n\in\mathcal{R}\),

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n)\le \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right). \tag{3.9} \] Bauer proves these from finite additivity + nonnegativity.

7 7. A premeasure gives “countable subadditivity” as well

Bauer proves a fundamental estimate:

If \(\mu\) is a premeasure on a ring \(\mathcal{R}\) and

\[ A_0 \subset \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \quad (A_0,A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{R}), \]

then

\[ \mu(A_0)\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \tag{3.10} \]

In particular, if \(\bigcup_n A_n\in\mathcal{R}\),

\[ \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right)\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \tag{3.10'} \]

This is a key “tool inequality” that you should expect to use constantly.

8 8. Theorem 3.2: \(\sigma\)-additivity ↔︎ continuity properties

Bauer introduces the monotone-set notations:

  • \(E_n \uparrow E\) means \(E_1\subset E_2\subset\cdots\) and \(E=\bigcup_n E_n\).
  • \(E_n \downarrow E\) means \(E_1\supset E_2\supset\cdots\) and \(E=\bigcap_n E_n\).

Then Bauer’s Theorem 3.2 says (for a content \(\mu\) on a ring \(\mathcal{R}\)):

    1. \(\mu\) is a premeasure
      \(\Longleftrightarrow\)
    1. Continuity from below: if \(A_n\uparrow A\) with \(A_n,A\in\mathcal{R}\), then
    \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(A_n)=\mu(A). \]

and moreover - (b) ⇒ (c) Continuity from above (with finiteness): if \(A_n\downarrow A\) and \(\mu(A_n)<\infty\), then

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(A_n)=\mu(A). \]

and - (c) \(\Longleftrightarrow\) (d) Continuity at 0: if \(A_n\downarrow\emptyset\) and \(\mu(A_n)<\infty\), then \(\mu(A_n)\to 0\).

Finally, if \(\mu\) is finite on \(\mathcal{R}\) (i.e. \(\mu(A)<\infty\) for all \(A\in\mathcal{R}\)), then all four statements (a)–(d) are equivalent.

8.1 Why this is conceptually important

In probability, measures are usually finite (\(P(\Omega)=1\)). In that setting, you can think:

“Countable additivity” is equivalent to saying the measure behaves well under monotone limits of sets.

This is the set-level analog of why Lebesgue integration has strong convergence theorems.

9 9. Definition 3.3: measure (the “final” object)

Bauer now defines the central concept:

A measure is a premeasure defined on a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\).
If \(\mu(\Omega)<\infty\), then \(\mu\) is a finite measure.

Equivalently: \(\mu:\mathcal{F}\to[0,+\infty]\) such that \(\mu(\emptyset)=0\) and \(\sigma\)-additivity holds for all disjoint sequences in \(\mathcal{F}\).

Bauer also highlights the mass distribution interpretation: \(\mu(A)\) is “mass smeared over \(A\),” and the Dirac measure concentrates all mass at a single point.

9.1 Standard measures mentioned right away

  • Zero measure: \(\mu\equiv 0\) on any \(\sigma\)-algebra.
  • Dirac measure: Example 1 becomes a measure when the domain is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
  • Counting measure: \(\zeta(A)=|A|\) if finite, else \(+\infty\), is a measure on \(\mathscr{P}(\Omega)\).
  • Example 2 becomes a measure because its domain is already a \(\sigma\)-algebra.

10 10. Lemma 3.4: a “bounded overlap” inequality

Bauer derives a consequence of \(\sigma\)-additivity that is stronger than it might look:

Let \(\mu\) be a measure on a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\), and let \((A_n)\subset\mathcal{F}\).
Assume there is \(k\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(A_m\cap A_n=\emptyset\) whenever \(|m-n|>k\) (i.e. sets far apart in index do not overlap).

Then

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n) \; \leq \; (k+1)\,\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right). \tag{3.12} \]

When \(k=1\), this reduces (essentially) to the \(\sigma\)-additivity requirement.

Interpretation. Even if the sets are not disjoint, as long as overlap is uniformly limited, you can still control the total mass of the sequence by the mass of the union.

11 11. Translating section 3 into probability / biostatistics language

  • A measurable space is \((\Omega,\mathcal{F})\): outcomes and measurable events.
  • A measure space is \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\): events plus a mass/probability assignment.
  • A probability measure is a measure \(P\) with \(P(\Omega)=1\) (Bauer later calls this a p-measure).

Why Dirichlet process (DP) talk is “measure talk”:

  • DP is a distribution over random probability measures.
  • So the output object of a DP is precisely a measure (\(\sigma\)-additive set function on a \(\sigma\)-algebra), not just a density or PMF.

12 12. Quick self-checks (to verify you “own” section 3)

You understand section 3 if you can do the following without guessing:

  1. Explain precisely why Example 3 is finitely additive but not \(\sigma\)-additive.
  2. State Theorem 3.2 and explain why in probability the “finite” case makes (a)–(d) equivalent.
  3. Given a set function \(\mu\), decide whether it is:
    • a content,
    • a premeasure,
    • a measure, by checking domain + additivity level.

### Example 3 (0–1 content on the finite/co-finite algebra; not a premeasure)

**Setting.** Let $\Omega$ be **countably infinite** (e.g. $\Omega = \mathbb{N}$). Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the algebra of sets that are **finite or co-finite** (Example 9 in section 1). 

Define

$$
\mu(A)=
\begin{cases}
0,& A\text{ is finite}\\
1,& A^c\text{ is finite (i.e. }A\text{ is co-finite)}.
\end{cases}
$$

Bauer: $\mu$ is a **content** but **not** a premeasure.

Let $A_1,\dots,A_n$ be pairwise disjoint sets in $\mathcal{A}$.

- If one of them, say $A_j$, is co-finite, then $A_j^c$ is finite and for $i\neq j$,
  $A_i\subset A_j^c$, hence $A_i$ is finite. So $\sum \mu(A_i)=1$ and the union is co-finite ⇒ $\mu(\cup A_i)=1$.

$$
\Omega=\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{\omega_n\}.

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(\{\omega_n\})=0.
$$

But $\bigcup_n \{\omega_n\}=\Omega$, and $\Omega$ is co-finite in itself (complement $\emptyset$ is finite), so $\mu(\Omega)=1$. Therefore

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{\omega_n\}\right)=1\neq 0=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(\{\omega_n\}).
$$

So (3.2) fails. Bauer explicitly points to exactly this decomposition as the reason.

## 6. Properties every content has (even before $\sigma$-additivity)

Bauer derives several identities/inequalities that any content $\mu$ on a ring satisfies. 

For $A,B,A_1,\dots\in\mathcal{R}$:

1) **Inclusion–exclusion (two-set form)**

$$
\mu(A\cup B)+\mu(A\cap B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B).
\tag{3.5}
$$

2) **Monotonicity (isotoneity)**

$$
A\subset B \;\Rightarrow\; \mu(A)\le \mu(B).
\tag{3.6}
$$

3) **Subtractivity** (when $\mu(A)<\infty$)

$$
A\subset B,\ \mu(A)<\infty \Rightarrow \mu(B\setminus A)=\mu(B)-\mu(A).
\tag{3.7}
$$

4) **Finite subadditivity**

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right)\le \sum_{i=1}^n \mu(A_i).
\tag{3.8}
$$

5) A one-sided inequality for disjoint sequences in a ring:
for disjoint $(A_n)\subset\mathcal{R}$ with $\bigcup_n A_n\in\mathcal{R}$,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n)\le \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right).
\tag{3.9}
$$
Bauer proves these from finite additivity + nonnegativity.

## 7. A premeasure gives “countable subadditivity” as well

Bauer proves a fundamental estimate:

If $\mu$ is a **premeasure** on a ring $\mathcal{R}$ and

$$
A_0 \subset \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \quad (A_0,A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{R}),
$$

then

$$
\mu(A_0)\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n).
\tag{3.10}
$$

In particular, if $\bigcup_n A_n\in\mathcal{R}$,

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right)\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n).
\tag{3.10'}
$$

This is a key “tool inequality” that you should expect to use constantly. 

---

## 8. Theorem 3.2: $\sigma$-additivity ↔ continuity properties

Bauer introduces the monotone-set notations:

- $E_n \uparrow E$ means $E_1\subset E_2\subset\cdots$ and $E=\bigcup_n E_n$.
- $E_n \downarrow E$ means $E_1\supset E_2\supset\cdots$ and $E=\bigcap_n E_n$. 

Then Bauer’s Theorem 3.2 says (for a **content** $\mu$ on a ring $\mathcal{R}$):

- (a) $\mu$ is a **premeasure**  
  $\Longleftrightarrow$
- (b) **Continuity from below:** if $A_n\uparrow A$ with $A_n,A\in\mathcal{R}$, then

  $$
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(A_n)=\mu(A).
  $$

and moreover
- (b) ⇒ (c) **Continuity from above** (with finiteness): if $A_n\downarrow A$ and $\mu(A_n)<\infty$, then
 
  $$
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(A_n)=\mu(A).
  $$

and
- (c) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (d) **Continuity at 0:** if $A_n\downarrow\emptyset$ and $\mu(A_n)<\infty$, then $\mu(A_n)\to 0$.

Finally, if $\mu$ is **finite** on $\mathcal{R}$ (i.e. $\mu(A)<\infty$ for all $A\in\mathcal{R}$), then **all four statements (a)–(d) are equivalent**. 

### Why this is conceptually important

In probability, measures are usually finite ($P(\Omega)=1$). In that setting, you can think:

> “Countable additivity” is equivalent to saying the measure behaves well under monotone limits of sets.

This is the set-level analog of why Lebesgue integration has strong convergence theorems.

---

## 9. Definition 3.3: measure (the “final” object)

Bauer now defines the central concept:

> A **measure** is a **premeasure** defined on a **$\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{F}$.  
> If $\mu(\Omega)<\infty$, then $\mu$ is a **finite measure**. 

Equivalently: $\mu:\mathcal{F}\to[0,+\infty]$ such that $\mu(\emptyset)=0$ and $\sigma$-additivity holds for all disjoint sequences in $\mathcal{F}$. 

Bauer also highlights the **mass distribution** interpretation: $\mu(A)$ is “mass smeared over $A$,” and the Dirac measure concentrates all mass at a single point. 

### Standard measures mentioned right away

- **Zero measure:** $\mu\equiv 0$ on any $\sigma$-algebra. 
- **Dirac measure:** Example 1 becomes a measure when the domain is a $\sigma$-algebra. 
- **Counting measure:** $\zeta(A)=|A|$ if finite, else $+\infty$, is a measure on $\mathscr{P}(\Omega)$. 
- **Example 2 becomes a measure** because its domain is already a $\sigma$-algebra.

---

## 10. Lemma 3.4: a “bounded overlap” inequality

Bauer derives a consequence of $\sigma$-additivity that is stronger than it might look:

Let $\mu$ be a measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$, and let $(A_n)\subset\mathcal{F}$.  
Assume there is $k\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $A_m\cap A_n=\emptyset$ whenever $|m-n|>k$ (i.e. sets far apart in index do not overlap).

Then

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n) \; \leq \; (k+1)\,\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right).
\tag{3.12}
$$

When $k=1$, this reduces (essentially) to the $\sigma$-additivity requirement. 

**Interpretation.** Even if the sets are not disjoint, as long as overlap is uniformly limited, you can still control the total mass of the sequence by the mass of the union.

---

## 11. Translating section 3 into probability / biostatistics language

- A **measurable space** is $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$: outcomes and measurable events.   
- A **measure space** is $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)$: events plus a mass/probability assignment.  
- A **probability measure** is a measure $P$ with $P(\Omega)=1$ (Bauer later calls this a p-measure).  

Why Dirichlet process (DP) talk is “measure talk”:

- DP is a distribution over **random probability measures**.  
- So the output object of a DP is precisely a measure ($\sigma$-additive set function on a $\sigma$-algebra), not just a density or PMF.
