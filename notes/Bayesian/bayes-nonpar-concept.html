Context

These notes are written so that you could teach from them. Nothing is introduced without motivation, and every mathematical choice is justified in terms of logic, decision‑making, and statistical learning.

The pedagogical principle is: At every step, the student should know

  1. what problem we are solving,
  2. why this tool is chosen, and
  3. what would break if we chose something else.

1 The Core Question of Statistics (Before Any Bayesian Language)

1.1 What Are We Actually Trying to Learn?

We observe data:

\[ X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n. \]

The most fundamental assumption in statistics is:

There exists some probability distribution \(F_0\) that generated these data.

This statement alone already contains everything:

  • There is uncertainty (we do not know \(F_0\))
  • There is structure (the data are not arbitrary)
  • There is a learning problem (use data to infer \(F_0\))

Every statistical method answers the same question:

How do we learn about the unknown data‑generating distribution?

1.2 Frequentist statistics

Assume \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d) from a fixed density \(f\)

This constraint is not okay!

If the true data-generating process is slightly skewed or heavy-tailed, the parametric model will never converge to the truth, regardless of sample size. It will converge to the “closest” Normal distribution.

It will forever remian biased.

2 Classical Approaches and Their Limitations

2.1 Parametric Models

In parametric statistics, we assume:

\[ F_0 \in \{F_\theta : \theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d\}. \]

This assumption is extremely strong.

What we gain:

  • Finite‑dimensional optimization
  • Fast convergence
  • Simple interpretation

What we lose:

  • Model correctness
  • Robustness
  • Ability to represent complex distributions
  • Exchangeability for parametric Bayesian

Teaching insight: Parametric models do not learn distributions — they learn parameters inside a rigid family.

2.2 Example of paramteric assumption

Sometimes we fake as if we know the answer.

e.g.) “I am certain the data generating mechanism is a Gaussian bell curve. I am only uncertain about its location (\(\mu\)) and scale (\(\sigma\)) <- Problem: Reduces the infinite complexity of the real world to a two-dimensional problem.

Problem?: such constraint is artificial and incohehent. Incoherent means that you ….. If the true data-generating process is slightly skewed or heavy-tailed, the parametric model will never converge to the truth, regardless of sample size. It will converge to the “closest” Normal distribution. It will forever remian biased

Bayesian Nonparametrics (BNP) removes this constraint. Because instead of putting prior probability mass on a vector of parameters \(\theta = (\mu , \sigma)\), it puts on the function space of all possible probability densities.

Parameter = density function \(f\) (can be of infinite degrees of freedom).

Truly let the data speak for itself.

2.3 Nonparametric (Frequentist) View

We drop the parametric assumption and attempt to estimate \(F_0\) directly:

  • Kernel density estimators
  • Empirical CDF
  • Sieve methods

But uncertainty quantification becomes difficult.

This motivates Bayesian thinking.

3 Bayesian Thinking: What Is Random?

3.1 Key Bayesian Shift

Bayesian statistics answers uncertainty by randomizing the unknown.

  • In parametric Bayes: \(\theta\) is random
  • In nonparametric Bayes: the distribution \(F\) is random

This is not philosophical — it is a modeling decision.

4 Treating the Distribution as Unknown (The Conceptual Leap)

4.1 What Does It Mean for a Distribution to Be Random?

Think operationally:

  1. Nature first selects a distribution \(F\)
  2. Then data are generated i.i.d. from that \(F\)

This is written as:

\[ F \sim \pi, \quad X_i \mid F \sim F. \]

Here:

  • \(\pi\) is a probability law whose outcomes are entire distributions

This answers a student’s natural question:

“How can we assign probability to something infinite‑dimensional?”

Answer: The same way we assign probability to random functions or stochastic processes — via a probability law on a space of objects.

5 Bayes’ Rule When the Unknown Is a Distribution

Bayes’ rule is structural:

\[ \text{posterior} \propto \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}. \]

Here:

  • Prior: \(\pi(df)\)
  • Likelihood: how well \(f\) explains observed data

So:

\[ \pi(df \mid X_{1}, X_2, \cdots, X_{n}) = \frac{ \text{likelihood } \cdot \text{ prior}}{ \text{marginal density of }X_i's} = \frac{ \prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i) \, \cdot \Pi(df)}{\int \prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i) \, \Pi(df)} \]

where

  • Numerator:
    • Likelihood = \(\prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i)\) = likelihood of the data under specific density \(f\).
    • prior = \(\Pi (df)\) = prior weight of that density.
  • Denominator:
    • The marginal likelihood (evidence), obtained by integrating over all possible functions \(f\).

This equation defines Bayesian nonparametrics. Everything else is analysis of its behavior.

Please refer to notes bayes-measure-d-concept.Rmd file to understand meaning of \(d\Pi(df)\).

Generalization to Function Spaces: Walker relies on the generalization of this theorem @ref{hewitt1955symmetric} to general variables. It states that any infinitely exchangeable sequence of random variables can be represented as a mixture of i.i.d. sequences.

\[p(x_1, \cdots , x_n) = \int \Big( \Pi_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i) \Big) d\Pi(f)\]

6 Learning Means Posterior Concentration

6.1 What Does “Learning the Distribution” Mean?

We do not expect the posterior to collapse to a single point exactly.

Instead, we want:

As \(n \to \infty\), the posterior puts most of its mass near the true distribution \(F_0\).

This raises an unavoidable question:

What does “near” mean for probability distributions?

This question forces the choice of a distance or divergence.

7 What “Bayesian Nonparametrics” Really Means

7.1 Parametric vs Nonparametric (Bayesian View)

In parametric Bayesian statistics, we assume:

  • A finite-dimensional parameter \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d\)
  • A likelihood \(p(x \mid \theta)\)
  • A prior \(\pi(\theta)\)

In Bayesian nonparametrics, the unknown object is not finite-dimensional. Instead, it is typically:

  • A probability distribution \(F\)
  • Or a function (density, regression function, hazard rate, etc.)

So the prior is not \(\pi(\theta)\), but \[ \pi(dF) \] which is a probability measure on a space of probability measures.

This is the key conceptual leap.

8 Bayesian Choices - The Incoherence of Constraints

8.1 The “What If” Question

Critics to nonparametricians: “What if the true density is normal? Won’t your infinite-dimensional model be inefficient?”

Critics to parametrician:

  • “What if the model is not normal?”
  • A parametric model (like the Normal distribution) puts probability zero on any shape that isn’t a bell curve.
  • If the true data has a slight skew or heavy tails, a normal model will never converge to the truth, no matter how much data you have.
  • The KL divergence will minimize to the “closest” normal, but the error will remain non-zero.

8.2 Incoherence of Post-Data Selection

Selecting a prior after looking at the data (e.g., checking if data looks normal, then choosing a normal prior) \(\rightarrow\) violates the axioms of probability.

  • Coherence: A statistical analysis is coherent if it follows the laws of probability based on information available before the decision.
  • The “Big” Prior: The solution is to define a prior support that is “large enough” to encompass all reasonable possibilities before seeing the data. This necessitates a nonparametric prior (one with support over the entire space of densities).

8.3 Specifying the Nonparametric Prior

How do we define a prior \(\Pi\) over infinite functions?

  • use moments
  • If we can specify expectations like \(E[f(x)]\) (the mean shape) and \(E[f^2(x)]\) (the variance of the shape), we can construct a valid nonparametric prior.

Example: We might center our nonparametric prior on a Normal distribution. This means our “best guess” is a bell curve, but we allow the posterior to deviate from this shape if the data demands it. This creates a “random density” that fluctuates around a parametric base.

Table 1. Parametric vs. Nonparametric Priors
Feature Parametric Prior (e.g., Normal) Nonparametric Prior (e.g., Dirichlet Process)
Parameter space Finite dimension (\(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^k\)) Infinite dimension (Function space \(\mathcal{F}\))
Flexibility Rigid; fixed shape assumption Flexible; adapts to any shape
Bias/Variance High Bias (if model wrong), Low Variance. Low Bias, Higher Variance (initially)
Convergence Converges to “closest” parametric fit. Converges to true density \(f_0\) (Consistency).
Philosophy “I know the shape, just not the size.” “I am uncertain about the shape itself.”

8.4 Common argument

“It’s not possible to learn about all the parameters of a nonparametric model.”

Walker’s note

Spectacularly misses the point. Bayesian inference is about being willing and able to speficy all uncertainties into a prior distribution. If noe does not like the outcome, do not be a Bayesian.

The “complete” (nonparametric) prior represents genuine uncertainty.

9 Decision Theory

In this section, Walker shows the complete prior framework using decision theory. He also shows how maximum likelihood estimate can be viewed as a decision problem within a nonparametric framework.

Example: Which specific density from a family of densities indexed by a parameter that is the best approximation to the data \(X_1, \cdots , X_n\)?

9.1 Setup

Denote:

  • \(f(\cdot)\): State of nature. The true but unknown density which generated the data. Infinite-dimensional. Treated as random under the posterior \(\Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\).
  • \(\theta\): Action. Decision parameter. We select a parameter \(\theta\). Not random.
  • \(f(\cdot; \, \theta)\): The density implied by that decision. A parametric approximation (a decision/action). A model-based candidate density chosen by the statistician.
    • Completely known once \(\theta\) is chosen.
    • not random
    • not the truth
    • lives in a parameteric family (e.g. Normal, Laplace, etc.)
    • This is a decision, not a random variable
  • \(\theta \in \Theta\): the specific parameter with which we can best approximate the data. e.g.) mean, variance, etc.
  • \(\{f(x; \, \theta)\}\) : the parametric family of densities characterized by \(\theta\).

choose a utility function which describes the reward in selecting \(\theta\) when \(f\) is the true density.

\[u : \text{(truth } f, \text{ decision } \theta) \mapsto \mathbb{R}\]

9.2 The Utility Function Approach

(Kullback-Leibler divergence) Let \(g\) and \(f\) be probability density functions of a random variable \(X \in \mathcal{X}\). Then, the distance between the two densities is:

\[d(g,f) = \int g(x) \; log \frac{g(x)}{f(x)} \, dx,\] with \(d(g,f) \geq 0\) and strictly equal to zero iff \(g=f\).

The utility is negative KL divergence:

\[u(f,\theta) = - d( \, f(\cdot), \; f(\cdot ; \theta) \, )\]

Interpretation:

“How much does the model \(f(\cdot ; \theta)\) disagree with the true density \(f\), when assessed under the weighting implied by the true density?”

Identify \(\theta\) that:

minimizes the distance \(\rightarrow\) maximize utility.

Q: So, how does nonparameric prior come into play?

Answer

Prior on \(f\). We go through all candidate \(f\) based on the data \(X_1, \cdots , X_n\).

9.3 The Utility function

We said put prior on \(f\)… So like this?

\[ \int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df)\] No. Let me just tell you this is wrong. What did I say? I say to put prior on the possible density \(f\) after observing the data!

So the correct answer is….

Answer

\[\int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\]

9.4 Maximization of Expected Utility

Let:

\[U_n(\theta) = \int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n),\] which is expected utility. Our goal is to find \(\theta\) which maximizes the expected utility. We can further show that maximizing \(U_n(\theta)\) is same as maximizing

\[\tilde{U}_n (\theta) = \int log \, f(x; \theta) \; f_n (dx) = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim f_n} \left[ log \, f( X ; \, \theta )\right],\] where \(f_n\) is the nonpaparmetric predictive density, given by

\[f_n(x)= \int f(x) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n).\]

So we can interpret \(\tilde{U}_n (\theta)\) as the posterior predictive expected log-likelihood of model \(\theta\). Or more simply, it’s posterior expectation of some loss/score.

We can read \(\tilde{U}_n(\theta)\) as: “How well does the parametric model \(f(\cdot; \, \theta)\) perform, on average, under my posterior beliefs about future data?”

Derivation of \(\tilde{U}_n (\theta)\) from \(U_n (\theta)\) 
\begin{aligned}
U_n(\theta) 
&= \int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \\
&= - \int d( \,   f(x) , \; f(x ; \theta)\, ) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\\
&= - \int \left(   \int f(x) \; log \frac{f(x)}{f(x ; \theta)} \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\\
&= - \int \left(   \int f(x) \; \left[log f(x) - log f(x; \theta) \right] \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\\
&= 

\underbrace{- \int \left(   \int f(x) \;log f(x) \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)}_{\text{independent of } \theta}
+
\underbrace{\int \left(   \int  f(x)  \;log f(x; \theta) \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)}_{\tilde{U}_n (\theta)}
\end{aligned}

And \(\tilde{U}_n (\theta)\) can be further simplified as :

\begin{aligned}
\tilde{U}_n (\theta)
&= \int log f(x; \, \theta) \left[ \int f(x) \, \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \right] dx, \quad log f(x; \, \theta) \text{ is fixed under } \Pi(df \mid X_{1:n}).\\
&= \int log f(x; \, \theta) \left[ f_n(x) \right] dx, \quad f_n(x) \text{ denotes posterior predictive distribution}\\
&=  \int log f(x; \, \theta)  f_n(dx), \quad  f_n(dx) \text{ is a measure theoric expression of } f_n(x)dx
\end{aligned}

Please refer to Section 11.1 to see a toy example of \(U_n(\theta).\)

9.5 Okay yeah so now what

In order to maximize \(\tilde{U}_n(\theta)\), we must know the posterior distribution, \(\Pi (df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\).

Estimate the posterior using Bayesian bootstrap.

Bayesian bootstrap posterior:

\[f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\, W_i \, \delta_{x_i}(x), \quad (W_1, ..., W_n) \sim Dirichlet(\alpha_1=1, ..., \alpha_n=1)\]

Note:

\begin{aligned}
  f_n(x) 
  &= \int f(x) \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \\
  &= \mathbb{E} [f(x) \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n] \\ 
  &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\,  W_i \, \delta_{x_i}(x) \right] \\
  &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[W_i]\delta_{x_i}(x), \quad \text{by linearity of expectation}\\
  &=  \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{n}\delta_{x_i}(x), \quad \mathbb{E}[W_i] = \left. \frac{\alpha_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_j} \right|_{\alpha_1=...\alpha_n=1} =  \frac{1}{n} .
\end{aligned}

Thus,

\begin{aligned}
  \tilde{U}_n(\theta)
  &= \int log f(x; \, \theta) f_n(dx)\\
  &=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} log f(x_i; \, \theta)
\end{aligned}

So \(\hat{\theta}\) is the maximum likelihood estimator!

We can also do this using each posterior draw of \(F\). Posterior expected uility collapses uncertainty via expectation. Instead, we may want to fully incorporate the uncertainty by evaluating each posterior draw of \(F\).

Draw \(F \sim \Pi( \cdot \mid X)\) evaludate utility under that draw. This yields: \[U^{(b)}(\theta) =\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i^{(b)} \, log f(X_i ; \theta).\]

By the end, we would have the distribution of the utility under posterior uncertainty.

Bayesian decision theory does not require collaping uncertainty immeidately, it’s just your choice.

10 Asymptotics

Question: If the data are truly i.i.d from \(f_0\), does the Bayesian posterior place more and more mass near \(f_0\) as \(n \rightarrow \infty\)?

For parametric Bayesian, it works… But under the assumption that the prior captures the true density. <- absurd to think this can happen.

For non-parametric bayesian, we don’t assume that. Prior is a random procedure that outputs a density.

Prior is not a formula - it is a random generator.

Histogram prior = random density generator.

  1. draw a partition
  2. draw bin heights

Algorithm: draw a random density \(f\)

Step 1: draw number of bins

  • sample \(k \in \{ 1, 2, 3, ... \}\) from some distribution
  • \(k\) is a model-complexity index, each value of \(k\) defines a family of densities

Step 2: Define bins

  • Partition [0,1] into \(k\) bins: \[ B_1 = [0, 1/k), B_2=[1/k, 2/k), \cdots, B_k = [(k-1)/k, 1]\]

Step 3: Draw bin probabilities

  • Sample \((p_1, \cdots, p_k) \sim Dirichlet(1, \cdots, 1)\)

Step 4: Define density

\[f(x) = k \cdot p_j \quad \text{if } x \in B_j\]

  1. Prior belief: ex.)Dirichlet (1,…,1) or Dirichle (1,2,3,..,0.5)
  2. learning model ex.)

Symbolically, for a distance \(d\) (Hellinger \(d_H\)):

\[\Pi(\{ f: d(f, f_0) > \epsilon\} \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \xrightarrow[n \rightarrow \infty]{\text{a.s. under } P_{f_0}}\] for some fixed \(\epsilon > 0\). where

  • \(\text{a.s. under } P_{f_0}\): almost sure (a.s) convergence under the true data law \(P_{f_0}\) (not the true density, but the true density for the observed data, and fixed \(f_0\)).

This property is called posterior consistency

11 Appendix

11.1 Toy example of understanding \(U_n(\theta)\)

11.1.1 1. Setup — objects and notation

  • State space of possible true densities: \(\mathcal F = \{f_1,f_2,f_3\}\).
  • Posterior over densities after observing data: \(\Pi_n\) with \[\Pi_n(f_1)=0.25,\quad \Pi_n(f_2)=0.35,\quad \Pi_n(f_3)=0.40.\]
  • Parametric action space: \(\Theta = \{\theta_A,\theta_B\}\) (note: fewer \(\theta\)’s than \(f\)’s).
  • For each action \(\theta_j\) we have a candidate density \(f(\cdot;\theta_j)\).

Goal: choose \(\theta\in\Theta\) to maximize posterior expected utility.

11.1.2 2. Utility function

We use KL-based utility between densities (Walker’s special case). For any true density \(f\in\mathcal F\) and action \(\theta\in\Theta\), define

\[ u(f,\theta) = -\mathrm{KL}\big(f\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big). \]

This rewards actions whose parametric density is close (in KL) to the true density.

11.1.3 3. Posterior expected utility (finite form)

The posterior expected utility for an action \(\theta\) is

\[ U_n(\theta) = \mathbb E_{f\sim\Pi_n}[u(f,\theta)] = \sum_{i=1}^3 \Pi_n(f_i)\,u(f_i,\theta). \]

Equivalently,

\[ U_n(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^3 \Pi_n(f_i)\,\mathrm{KL}\big(f_i\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big) = -0.25 \mathrm{KL}\big(f_1\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big) -0.35 \mathrm{KL}\big(f_2\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big) -0.40 \mathrm{KL}\big(f_3\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big)\]

The optimal decision is

\[ \theta_n^* = \arg\max_{\theta\in\{\theta_A,\theta_B\}} U_n(\theta). \]

11.1.4 4. Small numeric example (explicit values)

Fix the posterior weights (as above) and suppose the pairwise KL divergences are given by the following table (these numbers are illustrative):

true density \(f_i\) KL to \(f(\cdot;\theta_A)\) KL to \(f(\cdot;\theta_B)\)
\(f_1\) 0.20 0.45
\(f_2\) 0.30 0.25
\(f_3\) 0.10 0.50

Compute posterior expected utility for each \(\theta\) (remember utility is negative KL):

  • For \(\theta_A\):

\[ U_n(\theta_A) = -\big(0.25\cdot 0.20 + 0.35\cdot 0.30 + 0.40\cdot 0.10\big). \] Compute inside the parentheses:

  • \(0.25\cdot 0.20 = 0.050\)
  • \(0.35\cdot 0.30 = 0.105\)
  • \(0.40\cdot 0.10 = 0.040\)

Sum: \(0.050 + 0.105 + 0.040 = 0.195\).

Thus

\[ U_n(\theta_A) = -0.195. \]

  • For \(\theta_B\):

\[ U_n(\theta_B) = -\big(0.25\cdot 0.45 + 0.35\cdot 0.25 + 0.40\cdot 0.50\big). \] Compute inside the parentheses:

  • \(0.25\cdot 0.45 = 0.1125\)
  • \(0.35\cdot 0.25 = 0.0875\)
  • \(0.40\cdot 0.50 = 0.2000\)

Sum: \(0.1125 + 0.0875 + 0.2000 = 0.4000\).

Thus

\[ U_n(\theta_B) = -0.400. \]

Decision: compare utilities. Since \(-0.195 > -0.400\), we prefer \(\theta_A\). Therefore

\[ \theta_n^* = \theta_A. \]

Interpretation: although \(f_2\) and \(f_3\) give different KL behaviors, the posterior weights combined with their KL distances make \(\theta_A\) the better parametric approximation on average.

**What we gain:**

-   Finite‑dimensional optimization
-   Fast convergence
-   Simple interpretation

**What we lose:**

-   Model correctness
-   Robustness
-   Ability to represent complex distributions
-   Exchangeability for parametric Bayesian

> Teaching insight: Parametric models *do not learn distributions* —
> they learn parameters inside a rigid family.

## Example of paramteric assumption

Sometimes we fake as if we know the answer.

e.g.) "I am certain the data generating mechanism is a Gaussian bell
curve. I am only uncertain about its location ($\mu$) and scale
($\sigma$) \<- Problem: Reduces the infinite complexity of the real
world to a two-dimensional problem.

> Problem?: such constraint is artificial and incohehent. Incoherent
> means that you ..... If the true data-generating process is slightly
> skewed or heavy-tailed, the parametric model will never converge to
> the truth, regardless of sample size. It will converge to the
> "closest" Normal distribution. It will forever remian biased

Bayesian Nonparametrics (BNP) removes this constraint. Because instead
of putting prior probability mass on a vector of parameters
$\theta = (\mu , \sigma)$, it puts on the **function space** of all
possible probability densities.

Parameter = density function $f$ (can be of infinite degrees of
freedom).

Truly let the data speak for itself.

## Nonparametric (Frequentist) View

We drop the parametric assumption and attempt to estimate $F_0$

-   Kernel density estimators
-   Empirical CDF

But uncertainty quantification becomes difficult.

This motivates Bayesian thinking.

# Bayesian Thinking: What Is Random?

## Key Bayesian Shift

Bayesian statistics answers uncertainty by *randomizing the unknown*.

-   In parametric Bayes: $\theta$ is random
-   In nonparametric Bayes: **the distribution** $F$ is random

This is not philosophical — it is a modeling decision.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Treating the Distribution as Unknown (The Conceptual Leap)

## What Does It Mean for a Distribution to Be Random?

Think operationally:

1.  Nature first selects a distribution $F$
2.  Then data are generated i.i.d. from that $F$

This is written as:

$$
F \sim \pi, \quad X_i \mid F \sim F.
$$

Here:

-   $\pi$ is a probability law whose outcomes are **entire
    distributions**

This answers a student’s natural question:

> “How can we assign probability to something infinite‑dimensional?”

Answer: The same way we assign probability to random functions or
stochastic processes — via a probability law on a space of objects.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Bayes’ Rule When the Unknown Is a Distribution

Bayes’ rule is structural:

$$
\text{posterior} \propto \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}.
$$

Here:

-   Prior: $\pi(df)$
-   Likelihood: how well $f$ explains observed data

So:

$$
\pi(df \mid X_{1}, X_2, \cdots, X_{n}) = \frac{ \text{likelihood } \cdot \text{ prior}}{ \text{marginal density of }X_i's} = \frac{ \prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i) \,  \cdot \Pi(df)}{\int \prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i) \, \Pi(df)}
$$

where

-   Numerator:
    -   Likelihood = $\prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i)$ = likelihood of the data
        under specific density $f$.
    -   prior = $\Pi (df)$ = prior weight of that density.
-   Denominator:
    -   The marginal likelihood (evidence), obtained by integrating over
        all possible functions $f$.

This equation *defines* Bayesian nonparametrics. Everything else is
analysis of its behavior.

> Please refer to notes `bayes-measure-d-concept.Rmd` file to understand
> meaning of $d\Pi(df)$.

Generalization to Function Spaces: Walker relies on the generalization
of this theorem \@ref{hewitt1955symmetric} to general variables. It states
that any infinitely exchangeable sequence of random variables can be
represented as a mixture of i.i.d. sequences.

$$p(x_1, \cdots , x_n) = \int \Big( \Pi_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i) \Big) d\Pi(f)$$

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Learning Means Posterior Concentration

## What Does “Learning the Distribution” Mean?

We do **not** expect the posterior to collapse to a single point
exactly.

Instead, we want:

> As $n \to \infty$, the posterior puts most of its mass near the true
> distribution $F_0$.

This raises an unavoidable question:

> **What does "near" mean for probability distributions?**

This question *forces* the choice of a distance or divergence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# What “Bayesian Nonparametrics” Really Means

## Parametric vs Nonparametric (Bayesian View)

In **parametric Bayesian statistics**, we assume:

-   A *finite-dimensional* parameter
    $\theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d$
-   A likelihood $p(x \mid \theta)$
-   A prior $\pi(\theta)$

In **Bayesian nonparametrics**, the unknown object is **not
finite-dimensional**. Instead, it is typically:

-   A **probability distribution** $F$
-   Or a **function** (density, regression function, hazard rate, etc.)

So the prior is not $\pi(\theta)$, but $$
\pi(dF)
$$ which is a **probability measure on a space of probability
measures**.

This is the key conceptual leap.

# Bayesian Choices - The Incoherence of Constraints

## The "What If" Question

Critics to nonparametricians: "What if the true density is normal? Won't
your infinite-dimensional model be inefficient?"

Critics to parametrician:

-   "What if the model is not normal?"
-   A parametric model (like the Normal distribution) puts probability
    zero on any shape that isn't a bell curve.
-   If the true data has a slight skew or heavy tails, a normal model
    will never converge to the truth, no matter how much data you have.
-   The KL divergence will minimize to the "closest" normal, but the
    error will remain non-zero.

## Incoherence of Post-Data Selection

Selecting a prior after looking at the data (e.g., checking if data
looks normal, then choosing a normal prior) $\rightarrow$ violates the
axioms of probability.

-   Coherence: A statistical analysis is coherent if it follows the laws
    of probability based on information available *before* the decision.
-   The "Big" Prior: The solution is to define a prior support that is
    "large enough" to encompass all reasonable possibilities before
    seeing the data. This necessitates a nonparametric prior (one with
    support over the entire space of densities).

## Specifying the Nonparametric Prior

How do we define a prior $\Pi$ over infinite functions?

-   use moments
-   If we can specify expectations like $E[f(x)]$ (the mean shape) and
    $E[f^2(x)]$ (the variance of the shape), we can construct a valid
    nonparametric prior.

Example: We might center our nonparametric prior on a Normal
distribution. This means our "best guess" is a bell curve, but we allow
the posterior to deviate from this shape if the data demands it. This
creates a "random density" that fluctuates around a parametric base.

| Feature | Parametric Prior (e.g., Normal) | Nonparametric Prior (e.g., Dirichlet Process) |
|-------------------------|:---------------------------:|-----------------:|
| Parameter space | Finite dimension ($\theta \in \mathbb{R}^k$) | Infinite dimension (Function space $\mathcal{F}$) |
| Flexibility | Rigid; fixed shape assumption | Flexible; adapts to any shape |
| Bias/Variance | High Bias (if model wrong), Low Variance. | Low Bias, Higher Variance (initially) |
| Convergence | Converges to "closest" parametric fit. | Converges to true density $f_0$ (Consistency). |
| Philosophy | "I know the shape, just not the size." | "I am uncertain about the shape itself." |

: Table 1. Parametric vs. Nonparametric Priors

"It's not possible to learn about all the parameters of a nonparametric
model."

# Decision Theory

In this section, Walker shows the complete prior framework using decision theory. 
He also shows how maximum likelihood estimate can be viewed as a decision problem 
within a nonparametric framework. 

Example: Which specific density from a family of densities indexed 
by a parameter that is the best approximation to the data $X_1, \cdots , X_n$? 





## Setup 


Denote:

- $f(\cdot)$: State of nature. The true but unknown density which generated the data. Infinite-dimensional. Treated as random under the posterior $\Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)$.
- $\theta$: Action. Decision parameter. We select a parameter $\theta$. Not random.
- $f(\cdot; \, \theta)$: The density implied by that decision. A parametric approximation (a decision/action). A model-based candidate density chosen by the statistician. 
    - Completely known once $\theta$ is chosen.
    - not random
    - not the truth
    - lives in a parameteric family (e.g. Normal, Laplace, etc.)
    - This is a *decision*, not a random variable
- $\theta \in \Theta$: the specific parameter with which we can best approximate the data. e.g.) mean, variance, etc.
- $\{f(x; \, \theta)\}$ : the parametric family of densities characterized by $\theta$. 


choose a utility function which describes the reward in selecting $\theta$ when 
$f$ is the true density. 


$$u : \text{(truth } f, \text{ decision } \theta) \mapsto \mathbb{R}$$

## The Utility Function Approach


**(Kullback-Leibler divergence)** Let $g$ and $f$ be probability density functions 
of a random variable $X \in \mathcal{X}$. Then, the distance between the two 
densities is:

$$d(g,f) = \int g(x) \; log \frac{g(x)}{f(x)} \, dx,$$
with $d(g,f) \geq 0$ and strictly equal to zero iff $g=f$.



The utility is negative KL divergence:

$$u(f,\theta) = - d( \,  f(\cdot), \; f(\cdot ; \theta) \, )$$

**Interpretation:**

> "How much does the model $f(\cdot ; \theta)$ disagree with the true density 
$f$, when assessed under the weighting implied by the true density?"



Identify $\theta$ that:

<div align="center">
minimizes the distance $\rightarrow$ maximize utility.
</div>


Q: So, how does nonparameric prior come into play?

<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

> Prior on $f$. We go through all candidate $f$ **based on** the data $X_1, \cdots , X_n$.

</details>





## The Utility function



We said put prior on $f$... So like this?

$$ \int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df)$$
No. Let me just tell you this is wrong. What did I say? 
I say to put prior on the possible density $f$ **after** observing the data! 

So the correct answer is....


<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

$$\int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)$$

</details>



## Maximization of Expected Utility


Let: 

$$U_n(\theta) = \int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n),$$
which is expected utility. Our goal is to find $\theta$ which maximizes the 
expected utility. We can further show that maximizing $U_n(\theta)$ is same 
as maximizing 

$$\tilde{U}_n (\theta) = \int log \, f(x; \theta) \; f_n (dx) = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim f_n} \left[ log \, f( X ; \, \theta )\right],$$
where $f_n$ is the nonpaparmetric predictive density, given by 

$$f_n(x)= \int f(x) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n).$$

So we can interpret $\tilde{U}_n (\theta)$ as the posterior predictive expected log-likelihood of model $\theta$. Or more simply, it's posterior expectation of some loss/score.


We can read $\tilde{U}_n(\theta)$ as:
"How well does the parametric model $f(\cdot; \, \theta)$ perform, on average, under my posterior beliefs about future data?"



<details>
<summary><strong>Derivation of $\tilde{U}_n (\theta)$ from $U_n (\theta)$ </strong></summary>


``` math
\begin{aligned}
U_n(\theta) 
&= \int u(f, \theta) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \\
&= - \int d( \,   f(x) , \; f(x ; \theta)\, ) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\\
&= - \int \left(   \int f(x) \; log \frac{f(x)}{f(x ; \theta)} \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\\
&= - \int \left(   \int f(x) \; \left[log f(x) - log f(x; \theta) \right] \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)\\
&= 

\underbrace{- \int \left(   \int f(x) \;log f(x) \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)}_{\text{independent of } \theta}
+
\underbrace{\int \left(   \int  f(x)  \;log f(x; \theta) \, dx  \right) \; \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)}_{\tilde{U}_n (\theta)}
\end{aligned}
```

And $\tilde{U}_n (\theta)$ can be further simplified as :

``` math
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{U}_n (\theta)
&= \int log f(x; \, \theta) \left[ \int f(x) \, \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \right] dx, \quad log f(x; \, \theta) \text{ is fixed under } \Pi(df \mid X_{1:n}).\\
&= \int log f(x; \, \theta) \left[ f_n(x) \right] dx, \quad f_n(x) \text{ denotes posterior predictive distribution}\\
&=  \int log f(x; \, \theta)  f_n(dx), \quad  f_n(dx) \text{ is a measure theoric expression of } f_n(x)dx
\end{aligned}
```

</details>


Please refer to @sec-app-utility-example to see a toy example of $U_n(\theta).$


## Okay yeah so now what 


In order to maximize $\tilde{U}_n(\theta)$, we must know the posterior distribution, $\Pi (df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n)$.

Estimate the posterior using **Bayesian bootstrap**.

Bayesian bootstrap posterior:

$$f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\,  W_i \, \delta_{x_i}(x), \quad (W_1, ..., W_n) \sim Dirichlet(\alpha_1=1, ..., \alpha_n=1)$$

Note:

``` math
\begin{aligned}
  f_n(x) 
  &= \int f(x) \Pi(df \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \\
  &= \mathbb{E} [f(x) \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n] \\ 
  &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\,  W_i \, \delta_{x_i}(x) \right] \\
  &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[W_i]\delta_{x_i}(x), \quad \text{by linearity of expectation}\\
  &=  \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{n}\delta_{x_i}(x), \quad \mathbb{E}[W_i] = \left. \frac{\alpha_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_j} \right|_{\alpha_1=...\alpha_n=1} =  \frac{1}{n} .
\end{aligned}
```

Thus, 

``` math
\begin{aligned}
  \tilde{U}_n(\theta)
  &= \int log f(x; \, \theta) f_n(dx)\\
  &=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} log f(x_i; \, \theta)
\end{aligned}
```

So $\hat{\theta}$ is the maximum likelihood estimator!



> We can also do this using each posterior draw of $F$. Posterior expected uility collapses uncertainty via expectation. Instead, we may want to fully incorporate the uncertainty by evaluating each posterior draw of $F$.

> Draw $F \sim \Pi( \cdot \mid X)$
evaludate utility under that draw.
This yields:
$$U^{(b)}(\theta) =\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i^{(b)} \, log f(X_i ; \theta).$$

> By the end, we would have the *distribution* of the utility under posterior uncertainty. 

> Bayesian decision theory does not require collaping uncertainty immeidately, it's just your choice.

# Asymptotics

Question: If the data are truly i.i.d from $f_0$, does the Bayesian posterior place more and more mass near $f_0$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$?

For parametric Bayesian, it works... But under the assumption that the prior captures the true density. <- absurd to think this can happen.

For non-parametric bayesian, we don't assume that. Prior is a **random procedure** that outputs a density.

> Prior is not a formula - it is a random generator. 


Histogram prior = random density generator. 

1. draw a partition
2. draw bin heights


Algorithm: draw a random density $f$

Step 1: draw number of bins

- sample $k \in \{ 1, 2, 3, ... \}$ from some distribution
- $k$ is a model-complexity index, each value of $k$ defines a family of densities  

Step 2: Define bins

- Partition [0,1] into $k$ bins:
$$ B_1 = [0, 1/k), B_2=[1/k, 2/k), \cdots, B_k = [(k-1)/k, 1]$$

Step 3: Draw bin probabilities

- Sample $(p_1, \cdots, p_k) \sim Dirichlet(1, \cdots, 1)$

Step 4: Define density

$$f(x) = k \cdot p_j \quad \text{if } x \in B_j$$

1. Prior belief:
    ex.)Dirichlet (1,...,1) or Dirichle (1,2,3,..,0.5)
2. learning model
    ex.)



Symbolically, for a distance $d$ (Hellinger $d_H$):

$$\Pi(\{ f: d(f, f_0) > \epsilon\} \mid X_1, \cdots, X_n) \xrightarrow[n \rightarrow \infty]{\text{a.s. under } P_{f_0}}$$
for some fixed $\epsilon > 0$. where

- $\text{a.s. under } P_{f_0}$: almost sure (a.s) convergence under the true data law $P_{f_0}$ (not the true density, but the true density for the observed data, and fixed $f_0$).


This property is called **posterior consistency**

# Appendix


## Toy example of understanding $U_n(\theta)$ {#sec-app-utility-example}



### 1. Setup — objects and notation

- State space of possible true densities:  $\mathcal F = \{f_1,f_2,f_3\}$.
- Posterior over densities after observing data: $\Pi_n$ with
  $$\Pi_n(f_1)=0.25,\quad \Pi_n(f_2)=0.35,\quad \Pi_n(f_3)=0.40.$$
- Parametric action space: $\Theta = \{\theta_A,\theta_B\}$ (note: fewer $\theta$'s than $f$'s).
- For each action $\theta_j$ we have a candidate density $f(\cdot;\theta_j)$.

**Goal:** choose $\theta\in\Theta$ to maximize posterior expected utility.


### 2. Utility function

We use KL-based utility between densities (Walker’s special case). For any true density $f\in\mathcal F$ and action $\theta\in\Theta$, define

$$ u(f,\theta) = -\mathrm{KL}\big(f\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big). $$



This rewards actions whose parametric density is close (in KL) to the true density.




### 3. Posterior expected utility (finite form)

The posterior expected utility for an action $\theta$ is

$$ U_n(\theta) = \mathbb E_{f\sim\Pi_n}[u(f,\theta)] = \sum_{i=1}^3 \Pi_n(f_i)\,u(f_i,\theta). $$

Equivalently,

$$ U_n(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^3 \Pi_n(f_i)\,\mathrm{KL}\big(f_i\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big) = 
-0.25 \mathrm{KL}\big(f_1\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big) 
-0.35 \mathrm{KL}\big(f_2\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big)
-0.40 \mathrm{KL}\big(f_3\,\|\, f(\cdot;\theta)\big)$$

The optimal decision is

$$ \theta_n^* = \arg\max_{\theta\in\{\theta_A,\theta_B\}} U_n(\theta). $$


### 4. Small numeric example (explicit values)

Fix the posterior weights (as above) and suppose the pairwise KL divergences are given by the following table (these numbers are illustrative):

| true density $f_i$ | KL to $f(\cdot;\theta_A)$ | KL to $f(\cdot;\theta_B)$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $f_1$ | 0.20 | 0.45 |
| $f_2$ | 0.30 | 0.25 |
| $f_3$ | 0.10 | 0.50 |

**Compute posterior expected utility** for each $\theta$ (remember utility is negative KL):

- For $\theta_A$:

$$ U_n(\theta_A) = -\big(0.25\cdot 0.20 + 0.35\cdot 0.30 + 0.40\cdot 0.10\big). $$
Compute inside the parentheses:

- $0.25\cdot 0.20 = 0.050$
- $0.35\cdot 0.30 = 0.105$
- $0.40\cdot 0.10 = 0.040$

Sum: $0.050 + 0.105 + 0.040 = 0.195$.

Thus

$$ U_n(\theta_A) = -0.195. $$

- For $\theta_B$:

$$ U_n(\theta_B) = -\big(0.25\cdot 0.45 + 0.35\cdot 0.25 + 0.40\cdot 0.50\big). $$
Compute inside the parentheses:

- $0.25\cdot 0.45 = 0.1125$
- $0.35\cdot 0.25 = 0.0875$
- $0.40\cdot 0.50 = 0.2000$

Sum: $0.1125 + 0.0875 + 0.2000 = 0.4000$.

Thus

$$ U_n(\theta_B) = -0.400. $$

**Decision:** compare utilities. Since $-0.195 > -0.400$, we prefer $\theta_A$. Therefore

$$ \theta_n^* = \theta_A. $$

Interpretation: although $f_2$ and $f_3$ give different KL behaviors, the posterior weights combined with their KL distances make $\theta_A$ the better parametric approximation on average.
